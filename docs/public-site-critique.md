# Dr Fraction public-site critique and redesign direction

## The central diagnosis

Your instinct is right: the public site is **competent but underpowered as a publication**. It currently presents a credible person, their research, articles, webinars, and newsletter. That makes it read more like a professional portfolio or academic profile than a publication people return to.

The problem is not simply visual polish. The site lacks a strong answer to three reader questions:

1. **Why should I read this person rather than another expert?**
2. **Where should I begin?**
3. **Why should I come back?**

The strongest reference sites answer those questions through a visible editorial point of view, a curated archive, and a concrete audience relationship. The Marginalian makes authorship and curation central. Farnam Street turns a clear intellectual promise into a newsletter, archive, podcast, books, and membership. Aeon packages essays with a thesis, author credentials, reading time, editorial context, and related reading. Ness Labs uses a repeatable vocabulary and a clear problem-led promise. Paul Graham is visually austere, but the archive is made memorable by strong writing, direct recommendations, and obvious authorial continuity. [1] [2] [3] [4] [5]

The crucial distinction for Dr Fraction is that the **person is the durable product**. Malaria and clinical pharmacology may be important founding subjects, but the platform should hold future research, books, events, essays, teaching, cultural interests, and other work without feeling like a rebrand every time the subject changes. This is closer to a **person-led publishing house, intellectual studio, or personal media platform** than to a single-topic publication.

## What is already working

Dr Fraction already has several valuable foundations:

- A credible founding body of work: antimalarial medicines, drug resistance, clinical pharmacology, Malawi, and research ethics.
- A person with enough professional and intellectual range to become the organizing principle across future books, events, papers, essays, teaching, and other projects.
- A coherent editorial tone: restrained, serious, and text-led rather than loud.
- A usable article model with reading time, tags, references, sharing, bookmarking, citation, and newsletter signup.
- A research archive and paper-detail route rather than only a generic biography.
- A clear author identity and institutional context.
- A members-only access system, account area, comments, bookmarks, and newsletter infrastructure.

The existing typography—Newsreader for display and Inter for interface text—is not inherently a mistake. The current black, warm-white, and hairline-border system is also not inherently weak. It becomes weak because it is asked to carry the whole identity without enough **content hierarchy, image direction, curation, or reader pathway**.

## Where the current public site loses force

### 1. The homepage starts with the professional identity, not the reader’s reason to stay

The homepage opens with the affiliation, the line “Clinical pharmacology for better malaria treatment,” a general description, and two broad buttons: “Read the research” and “About.” This is credible, but it is close to institutional profile language and makes the platform sound narrower than the person’s future ambitions.

The visitor does not yet see:

- the most useful or surprising current idea;
- a hand-picked route for a newcomer;
- what kind of essays are published here;
- what a reader will understand better after ten minutes;
- why this is a distinct voice rather than an online CV.

A stronger opening would introduce the person’s worldview and range, then offer several entry routes: current thinking, research, events, books, and selected writing. It should make the platform feel alive before asking the visitor to understand the whole career.

### 2. The homepage is a sequence of departments

The current homepage moves from hero to articles, webinars, selected research, about, and newsletter. Each section is individually reasonable. Together, they feel like a list of site functions: articles, events, papers, biography, email.

That is why the result can feel like a portfolio even when the styling is thoughtful. The page is organized around **what the owner has**, not around **what the reader wants to do next**.

A more editorial sequence would be:

1. What Dr Fraction is trying to understand or change.
2. Three hand-picked starting pieces.
3. The latest essay or research note.
4. A thematic path such as resistance, treatment, evidence, or ethics.
5. Selected research outputs.
6. A real newsletter sample and signup.
7. About/method and contact.

Webinars should be a supporting route, not a competing primary identity unless they are the main audience engine.

### 3. The visible image treatment is currently unfinished

The homepage exposes the instruction “Replace `/covers/field-notes.png` with a portrait or lab photo.” That is an implementation note, not public-facing editorial content. It immediately lowers trust and makes the site look scaffolded.

This is probably the single most visible polish problem. Do not solve it with generic stock photography or AI-generated “African healthcare” imagery. Use one of the following instead:

- a real, well-lit portrait;
- a real field, laboratory, microscopy, ward, notebook, or teaching image with permission and credit;
- a restrained commissioned illustration based on a real research object;
- a simple diagram or annotated research image when it carries meaning.

The image system should have a point of view. It should not simply decorate cards.

### 4. The article template is credible but too thin before and after the essay

The article page currently gives the reader a back link, title, author name, date, reading time, tags, cover image, body, references, sharing, bookmarking, citation, engagement, and newsletter signup.

That is a sound technical base. It is missing several editorial trust and discovery layers:

- a strong dek/excerpt under the title;
- author credentials or a short “why this matters” note;
- a visible topic or series context;
- source/method information when appropriate;
- revision date when an article has materially changed;
- related reads with a reason for each recommendation;
- a next article or thematic path;
- a small, human author note rather than only a name;
- a public sample/archive route for the newsletter.

Aeon’s essay template is useful here because it makes topic, dek, author credentials, editor credit, date, word count, listening option, and related content part of the reading object. The Marginalian does something different but equally valuable: source links, captions, context, and related essays are woven into the editorial experience. [2] [5]

### 5. The archive is a feed, not yet a library

The blog listing currently provides a chronological list and up to twelve tag filters. That is a start, but it does not yet feel like a body of work.

The references consistently separate multiple discovery modes:

- **Start Here** for newcomers;
- **Latest** for returning readers;
- **Popular/Most read** for social proof and efficient discovery;
- **Topics or concepts** for browsing by question;
- **Related Reads** for continuity after an essay;
- **Archive** for depth and dated history.

Dr Fraction should not copy all of these at once. A small, curated version would be stronger: Start Here, Latest, Topics, and perhaps Most read once there is enough real data.

### 6. The newsletter promise is too general

“Occasional, considered emails” is tasteful but not specific enough to drive a habit. The visitor does not know the cadence, format, or benefit.

The reference sites make the newsletter a product:

- Farnam Street promises a five-minute weekly email with a clear intellectual benefit. [4]
- The Marginalian separates a new-essay digest from an archive re-read and publishes a sample issue. [2]
- Aeon offers Daily and Weekly options and clearly states that the content is free. [5]
- Ness Labs promises a weekly email with a defined practical point of view and repeats that promise throughout the site. [6]

Dr Fraction should choose one job first. It should be broad enough to hold the person’s changing work, but specific enough to become a habit. For example:

> **A weekly letter from Dr Fraction: ideas, research, books, events, and the questions connecting them.**

Then show a real sample issue. Do not ask for an email before explaining what arrives.

### 7. The members-only decision conflicts with visitor acquisition

This is the most important strategic issue.

The current product decision makes `/blog`, `/papers`, `/webinars`, and article pages members-only. That protects content and supports accounts, but it also removes the normal discovery loop used by the reference publications. The Marginalian, Paul Graham, Farnam Street, Aeon, and Ness Labs all use substantial public content to earn trust before asking for a newsletter, donation, account, or membership. [2] [3] [4] [5] [6]

If attracting visitors is a priority, I recommend one of these models:

**Recommended: public reading, account-based participation.**

- Public essays and paper abstracts.
- Sign-in required for bookmarks, comments, saved reading lists, and personalized newsletter settings.
- Newsletter signup remains available without an account.
- Members get convenience and participation features, not the first layer of intellectual access.

**Alternative: public preview, members-only full text.**

- Public title, dek, author, abstract, selected opening, references preview, and metadata.
- Sign-in required for the full essay.
- This is more protective, but it creates more friction and makes the public site less useful as a publication.

If the content must remain fully gated, the site needs a much stronger public layer: Start Here, sample essays, paper abstracts, newsletter archive, public talks, and a clear explanation of why membership exists.

## The design direction I recommend

### “The Dr Fraction publishing house”

Do not turn Dr Fraction into a generic magazine, a fake scientific journal, or a copied editorial template. The strongest original direction is a **human, field-based scholarly publication**.

Its visual character could be built from:

- one intentional display typeface and one highly readable text/UI face;
- a restrained warm paper base;
- one signature accent owned by the person/house, not permanently tied to malaria or a single discipline;
- project labels used sparingly: Research, Essays, Books, Events, Notes, Teaching, or other genuinely active strands;
- real photography, diagrams, book covers, event imagery, or commissioned illustrations with captions and credits;
- strong black text and generous reading measure;
- one recurring visual motif that belongs to the work—perhaps a line, grid, specimen mark, or annotated margin—but not decorative “science” graphics.

The goal is not to make every page look like a lab report or a personal CV. It is to make the site feel like a person who works across projects, thinks carefully, and invites readers into the reasoning, work, and opportunities around that person.

The information architecture should therefore be **person-first, project-aware**:

- **Home** — the current point of view and what deserves attention now;
- **Writing** — essays, notes, reflections, and public thinking;
- **Research** — papers, methods, findings, and scholarly outputs;
- **Books** — authored, edited, recommended, or forthcoming books;
- **Events** — talks, webinars, lectures, launches, and participation opportunities;
- **Projects** — temporary or cross-disciplinary initiatives;
- **About / Work with me** — biography, credentials, collaborations, speaking, teaching, and contact.

These should not all be empty top-level routes on day one. The navigation should expose only active areas while preserving a system that can grow without being redesigned.

## Anti-AI-slop rules for the redesign

These should be non-negotiable:

1. **No generic hero language.** Every headline should make a specific promise or pose a real question.
2. **No fabricated proof.** No invented reader counts, testimonials, comments, publications, or engagement numbers.
3. **No placeholder imagery in production.** If there is no suitable real image, use a deliberate typographic or diagrammatic treatment instead.
4. **No decorative gradients, glass cards, noisy badges, or excessive rounded pills.** Every visual element must clarify a task or support the editorial voice.
5. **No copy-pasted reference patterns.** Borrow mechanisms, not logos, colors, title formulas, or visual identity.
6. **No random “related posts.”** Every recommendation should have a reason: same concept, contrasting evidence, next step, or foundational context.
7. **No “academic” as an unsupported adjective.** Show sources, credentials, method, limits, and revision history where they matter.
8. **No intrusive growth mechanics.** No forced signup before a visitor can understand the work, no fake urgency, and no repeated popups.
9. **No excessive motion.** The current blur-and-rise reveal effect should become either a subtle opacity/translate transition or be removed from reading-critical content.
10. **Real content before component polish.** Design the first three to six real essays and their relationship to each other before building a large card system.

## Recommended redesign sequence

### Phase 0 — editorial decisions

Before changing the homepage, decide:

- Primary audience: clinical researchers, health workers, students, general readers, or a deliberate combination.
- Public access model: public reading, public preview, or fully members-only.
- The one-sentence editorial promise.
- Three to six real starting essays or planned exemplars.
- The first newsletter format and cadence.
- What Dr Fraction will consistently refuse to publish.

### Phase 1 — public shell and homepage

- Rework the navigation around Essays/Notes, Research, Start Here, About/Method, Newsletter, and Search.
- Replace the portfolio-style hero with a shorter editorial proposition and a real featured essay.
- Add a Start Here section with three to six curated entries.
- Separate Latest from curated work.
- Reduce the prominence of webinars unless they are the main acquisition channel.
- Replace the public placeholder image with a real or intentionally image-free treatment.

### Phase 2 — article experience

- Add dek, author credentials, topic/series, date, reading time, and optional word count.
- Improve source/method and revision presentation.
- Add deliberate Related Reads and a next-path module.
- Move the newsletter/support action after the reader has received value.
- Preserve citations, references, bookmarking, sharing, and accessibility.

### Phase 3 — archive and discovery

- Add Start Here.
- Add Latest and Topics.
- Add Most read only when there is enough real data.
- Add search when the archive justifies it.
- Add RSS and a browsable newsletter archive.
- Keep taxonomy small and editorially meaningful.

### Phase 4 — visual direction and content assets

- Replace the field-notes placeholder with a real image system.
- Establish the accent color, type scale, image crops, captions, and credit rules.
- Create a small set of article cover templates that are authored and repeatable, not a gallery of unrelated graphics.
- Test the system on a long essay, a paper, a webinar, and a no-image article.

### Phase 5 — audience and measurement

- Define the newsletter promise and publish a sample.
- Add a quiet footer and end-of-article signup.
- Measure engaged reading, return visits, newsletter conversion, and archive paths—not only pageviews.
- Add support or sponsorship only after the editorial proposition is clear.

### Phase 6 — responsive and quality review

Validate actual phone, tablet, and desktop renders for:

- navigation collapse and focus order;
- long titles and deks;
- image cropping and captions;
- citation/reference behavior;
- newsletter forms;
- reading measure;
- keyboard navigation;
- touch targets;
- reduced motion;
- loading and performance.

The supplied CMS image is a desktop screenshot. Its mobile behavior cannot be inferred from the pixels. The reference sites that document responsive behavior use explicit mobile menus, breakpoint-specific controls, stacked content, and simplified side rails; those behaviors should be tested in Dr Fraction rather than assumed. [1] [2] [4] [5] [6]

## My blunt recommendation

Do not start by making the current homepage “prettier.” Start by deciding whether Dr Fraction is primarily:
- a professional profile with supporting content;
- a single-topic publication;
- a person-led publishing house and intellectual studio;
- a members-only research community; or
- a public platform with member participation.

Based on your clarification, I recommend **a public person-led publishing house with member participation**. The content categories can expand. The durable promise is access to Dr Fraction’s way of thinking, making, researching, teaching, convening, and publishing.

Then make the public site earn attention through three things:

1. a distinctive, human promise centered on the person rather than one subject;
2. a curated body of work organized into active projects and strands;
3. a trustworthy reason to return for the next idea, event, book, essay, or opportunity.

Once those are correct, the visual redesign becomes much easier. It will be possible to make the site more memorable without resorting to generic “premium” design language.

## Questions to settle before implementation

1. Who is the primary reader we should design for first?
2. Should essays and research abstracts become publicly readable, with accounts reserved for bookmarks/comments/newsletter preferences?
3. Which three to six real pieces should form the first Start Here set?
4. Do you have real portrait, field, lab, teaching, microscopy, or conference images we can use?
5. Should the voice lean more toward personal field notes, public scholarship, or practical clinical interpretation?
6. What newsletter cadence can you realistically sustain?
